Low-AoI Data Collection for UA V-Assisted IoT
With Dynamic Geohazard Importance Levels
Xiuwen Fu, Member, IEEE ,T i a n l eW a n g , Pasquale Pace ,Member, IEEE ,
Gianluca Aloi ,Member, IEEE , and Giancarlo Fortino ,Fellow, IEEE
Abstract —After geohazards occur, conducting rapid and sus-
tainable secondary geohazard monitoring plays a crucial role
in reducing secondary geohazard risks. However, geohazard
situations vary across different areas and dynamically changewith the development of geohazards. Therefore, ensuring timelydata collection and the ability to dynamically adjust to changes ingeohazards poses signiﬁcant challenges in geohazard monitoringscenarios. This article proposes a low-latency data collectionscheme considering data importance levels (LLDCL), whichprioritizes data collection from high-importance sensor nodes(SNs) while still collecting data from lower importance SNs.Given the potential for sudden events in geohazard monitoringscenarios that may require adjustments to the emergency levelsof monitoring points, this article introduces a deep reinforce-ment learning (DRL) algorithm for unmanned aerial vehicles(UA Vs) path planning based on weighted age of information(DRL-WAoI). This algorithm enables UA Vs to respond quicklyto dynamic environments by adjusting their ﬂight paths inreal time. Furthermore, considering the limited battery capacityof UA Vs, this article establishes a token-based energy tradingmodel between UA Vs and the base station (BS) to facilitateUA V recharging. Simulation experiments show that the LLDCLscheme can effectively adapt to the dynamically changing con-ditions of geohazard monitoring scenarios, providing a viablesolution for UA V data collection and transmission.
Index Terms —Age of Information, data collection. deep rein-
forcement learning (DRL), geohazard monitoring, path planning,unmanned aerial vehicles (UA Vs).

risk and damage caused by secondary geohazards [1]. Primary
monitoring is typically carried out immediately after the
geohazard to assess damage and develop rescue plans, whilesecondary monitoring focuses on continuously tracking and
evaluating potential risks as the disaster evolves. unmanned

sensor nodes (SNs) in the affected area and collect data fromthese SNs to obtain information about the geohazard, aiding

Compared to primary monitoring, secondary monitoring
places greater emphasis on the timeliness of data. Excessive
data latency can reduce the quality and signiﬁcance of thecollected data, making it essential to minimize the latency
of important data as much as possible [4]. Additionally,
since secondary geohazards develop dynamically, the IoTsystem needs to continuously adjust its monitoring strategy in
response to changes in the geohazard situation. For example, in
the early stages of a geohazard, comprehensive data collection
and monitoring of all geohazard monitoring points within
the task area are necessary due to limited knowledge ofthe local geohazard situation. However, once a preliminary
understanding of the geohazard is obtained, the focus can shift
to monitoring key regions. Furthermore, if new emergenciesarise, the IoT system need to readjust the monitoring plan to
adapt to the new circumstances.
Despite signiﬁcant development in UA V-assisted IoT
systems, there are still many challenges in achieving dynamic
response and low-latency data collection in the geohazard
monitoring scenarios mentioned above.
1) Due to the limited battery capacity of UA Vs, a single
UA V cannot access all dispersed SNs in one task when
the collection area is large. Therefore, multiple UA Vsneed to work together to complete data collection from
all SNs.
2) Increasing the frequency of data collection from SNs
reduces data latency, thereby enhancing the timeli-
ness of environmental monitoring and improving thelikelihood of early hazard detection. However, not all
SNs contribute equally to the monitoring process. SNs
located in critical areas or closely related to the mon-itored geohazard provide more valuable and actionable
data. Therefore, to improve monitoring in these key
regions, it is crucial to prioritize data collection fromhigh-importance SNs. By assigning a higher collection
frequency to these priority nodes, we can minimize
data latency in critical areas, enabling more real-timedetection of environmental changes and ensuring a more

3) Due to the uncertainties in geohazard monitoring scenar-

Based on the challenges mentioned above, this article
proposes a low-latency data collection scheme considering
data importance levels (LLDCL), which achieves dynamic
path planning and efﬁcient charging for UA Vs in geohazard
monitoring scenarios. The main contributions of this article
are as follows.
1) To address the dynamic and sudden nature of geo-
hazards, a LLDCL is proposed. This scheme assigns
different importance levels to SNs based on the geo-
hazard situation in different areas. By guiding UA Vs
to prioritise data collection from SNs with higher
importance levels, and rationally allocating the ﬂight
and charging times of UA Vs, the system can achievelow-latency data collection in response to the dynamic
changes in the geohazard situation.
2) To enable UA Vs to autonomously make decisions and
dynamically plan ﬂight paths in response to changing
geohazard situations, a deep reinforcement learning
(DRL) algorithm for UA Vs path planning based on
weighted Age of Information (AoI), named “DRL-WAoI”, is proposed. The algorithm uses weighted AoI
to guide multiple UA Vs to collect data from SNs with
different importance levels and generate ﬂight paths of
UA Vs. To achieve efﬁcient charging of UA Vs, a token-
based energy trading model between UA Vs and the base
station (BS) is proposed, where UA Vs collect data from
SNs in exchange for charging tokens.
3) Through extensive simulations, the proposed data col-
lection scheme has been validated to ensure both
low latency and the importance of data. Under this
scheme, UA Vs can make autonomous decisions accord-
ing to changes in the geohazard situation, thus enablingdynamic path planning and efﬁcient charging for UA Vs.
The proposed system has been validated as effective
and superior by comparison with the latest UA V data
collection systems.

ITERATURE REVIEW
As a rapidly deployable and efﬁcient data collection scheme,
UA V-assisted IoT systems are widely applied in emergencyrescue scenarios, such as geohazards, ﬂoods, and forest ﬁres.
These systems enable emergency rescue personnel to obtain
timely and accurate data to support rescue decision making.Two main aspects of UA V-assisted IoT have attracted consid-
erable attention: 1) data collection and 2) charging scheduling.
In this section, we summarize the related literature in thesetwo areas.
The ﬂight path planning of UA Vs is critical to the
performance of the entire data collection system. Many
researchers have focused on optimizing efﬁciency and min-
imizing delays in this process. Chen et al. [5]designed a
clustering-based algorithm that categorizes a limited number
of areas into different clusters. They used mixed integer
linear programming to determine the ﬂight path of UA Vs,thereby minimizing the task completion time of UA Vs.
Han et al. [6]proposed a two-layer optimization approach to
optimize UA Vs deployment with a novel encoding strategy.They used an improved dandelion algorithm to plan UA Vs
ﬂight. Liu et al. [7]proposed a multipopulation genetic
algorithm combined with convex optimization techniques tojointly optimize task assignment, waypoint selection, and ﬂight
paths of UA Vs. This approach achieved the minimization of
AoI for data collection by users. Zhou et al. [8]used the
monarch butterﬂy optimization algorithm for UA Vs’ ﬂight
path planning and introduced a UA V-assisted sleep scheduling
algorithm to ensure network coverage while extending networklifetime. Wang et al. [9]proposed a heuristic algorithm using
adaptive large neighborhood search for multiperiod UA Vs
path planning. Yi et al. [10] proposed a UA V-assisted data
collection algorithm based on DRL. By employing a Markov
decision process (MDP), they identiﬁed the optimal ﬂightpaths for UA Vs and the transmission scheduling for sensors,
achieving a weighted minimization of AoI. Feng et al. [11]
proposed a joint path planning and resource scheduling schemefor UA Vs. They introduced an intelligent prediction mech-
anism to forecast the service demands of emergency area
devices and established a dynamic path planning scheme.Seid et al. [12] proposed a model-free DRL-based collabo-
rative computation ofﬂoading and resource alposition scheme
in an air-to-ground network for emergency situations. Byusing adaptive learning to derive efﬁcient solutions from
dynamic A2G networks, the scheme minimizes task execution
delays and energy consumption. Liu et al. [13] proposed a
distributed multiagent DRL solution that utilizes convolutional
neural networks to extract spatial features and integrates a
distributed prioritized experience replay. Abedin et al. [14]
proposed an agile DRL model based on experience replay,
along with an efﬁcient real-time paths strategy for UA Vs.Oubbati et al. [15] proposed a DRL framework to improve
UA V performance in terms of energy consumption, coverage
and routing efﬁciency. Sun et al. [16] proposed a UA Vs path
planning algorithm based on twin delayed deep deterministic
policy gradient. This algorithm optimizes UA V ﬂight speeds,
hovering positions, and data collection bandwidth allocation

UA V charging scheduling decisions, thereby reducing sensor

method to optimize UA V routing and developed a scenario-based UA V charging station planning stochastic optimization
model, which reduces the investment costs of UA V ﬂeets
under random delivery demands and improves operationalefﬁciency. Salameh et al. [31] designed a RL-based UA V
trajectory algorithm, which searches for appropriate locations
of available charging stations within the area and intelligently
plans UA V movement and ﬂight decisions to enhance detection
and tracking performance.

scenarios, the data collected by all SNs do not show signiﬁcant
differences in importance. However, in geohazard monitoring
scenarios, the varying emergency levels of different monitoredareas result in differing importance levels for SNs across
regions, and these levels can change as the geohazard situation
evolves. Therefore, in geohazard monitoring scenarios, UA Vsshould be able to make quick and appropriate responses based
on the current importance levels of SNs during data collection.
This speciﬁc task requirement makes existing UA V-assistedIoT data collection schemes less satisfactory in geohazard
monitoring scenarios. Therefore, to achieve efﬁcient operation
of UA V-assisted IoT in geohazard monitoring scenarios, we
need to address the following challenges: 1) how to design
UA Vs data collection strategies to enable coordinated cooper-ation between UA Vs; 2) how to assign and dynamically adjust
the importance levels of SNs in response to changes in the
geohazard situation; 3) how to generate UA Vs ﬂight paths andenable real-time adjustments of these paths to accommodate
changes in the importance levels of SNs; and 4) how to
design UA Vs charging strategies to minimize the impact ofthe charging process on data collection. These challenges
mentioned above are the main focus of this study.

vast and inaccessible to vehicles or personnel. In suchenvironments, traditional methods of investigation become

the UA Vs carry the SNs, transport them to the predeﬁned
locations, and deploy them into the monitoring area throughaerial dropping. Once deployed, the SNs operate indepen-
dently to monitor geohazards. At the same time, we consider
that environmental obstacles (e.g., trees, buildings, or uneventerrain) may interfere with communication links between SNs,
leading to signal attenuation, blockage, or even fragmentation

B. Sensor Node Settings
Geohazards are more likely to occur in regions with fragile
geological environments and relatively poor slope stability.
These regions are considered potential geohazard risk regions
and therefore require special attention. Geohazard monitoring
points are speciﬁcally designated locations used to monitor and
assess potential geohazard risks within these regions. Typicallyequipped with sensors and devices, these monitoring points
collect real-time data on geological conditions, rainfall, soil
moisture, and other critical factors, providing a scientiﬁc basisfor informed decision making [35]. Based on the insights
of geological experts in [9]regarding the classiﬁcation and
grading of monitoring conditions, this article categorizesgeohazard monitoring points into four emergency levels. This
classiﬁcation considers characteristics, such as the types of
geohazards, geological stability conditions, and threat ranges.Level I indicates a normal emergency status, while Level IV
represents the highest emergency level. Table IIshows the
relationship between the current stability status and dangerlevels of geohazard monitoring points and their corresponding
emergency levels. The meanings of the different emergency
levels are as follows.
1)Level I: Stable, indicating that there are no signiﬁcant
risks in the region, suitable for normal operations.
2)Level II: Caution, indicating some potential risks that
require moderate attention.
3)Level III: Unstable, indicating relatively high risks that
necessitate enhanced monitoring and early warning.
4)Level IV: Extremely unstable, indicating very high risks
that require immediate response measures.

and poor controllability when UA Vs drop SNs, we adopt theFig. 2. Correlation between geohazard monitoring points and importance

In this study, we assume that future advancements in RF

While UA Vs could indeed return to the BS for battery
replacement or contact charging, we argue that wireless charg-
ing offers distinct advantages in speciﬁc scenarios, particularly
in remote or hard-to-reach areas. First, wireless charging
eliminates the need for physical contact between the UA V

OW-LATENCY DATA COLLECTION SCHEME
A. Framework of LLDCL Scheme
This article proposes a LLDCL to address the dual require-
ments of low latency in UA V data collection and rapid
response to geohazards. The speciﬁc process of this schemeis shown in Fig. 4. First, to reduce the complexity of the
task, after each update of the emergency level of geohazard
monitoring points, we use a fuzzy threshold-based Gaussian
mixture algorithm to divide the sensor network deployed at
the geohazard monitoring points into clusters and determinecluster head nodes. UA Vs collect data from each cluster head
node to obtain data from SNs of the entire cluster and then
establish a long-distance transmission channel with the groundBS to upload this data [46]. During the data collection process,
if UA Vs lack sufﬁcient energy to complete the task, they canreturn to the BS for recharging. To achieve ﬂexible and efﬁ-
cient recharging of UA Vs, we model the recharging behavior
between UA Vs and the BS as a negotiated energy tradingmodel. UA Vs engage in energy transactions with the BS based
on their immediate needs, using a game theory model to
simultaneously enhance the utility and revenue of UA Vs. Inresponse to the dynamic environment where the importance
levels of SNs may change, we propose a DRL-WAoI. This
algorithm guides a group of UA Vs to collect SN data fromdispersed cluster head nodes, achieving the tasks of data
collection, data uploading, and UA V recharging by minimizing
the weighted AoI and considering UA V energy constraints.Through these steps, the ﬂight paths and recharging times of
UA Vs are jointly optimized.
It should be noted that our proposed scheme relies on the
precise position information of ground SNs. Therefore, during
the preparation phase of the task, UA Vs need to traverse theentire task area to collect this position information of SNs.
Subsequently, UA Vs can perform subsequent data collection
tasks based on the position information of SNs. UA Vs onlyneed to traverse the task area again and update the position
information of SNs when there is a change in the status of the
sensor network.
B. Clustering of Sensor Nodes
To enhance the efﬁciency of UA V data collection, we
recluster the sensor network following each update of theemergency levels at geohazard monitoring points. Within
each cluster, one SN is selected as the cluster head node,
responsible for collecting data from other SNs and uploadingit to the UA Vs when they come within communication range.
During the clustering process, we consider two situations: ﬁrst,
isolated SNs, which form independent clusters with the SNitself serving as the cluster head node and the second, for
sensor network subnets, we select the SN closest to the BS as
the cluster head node to minimize the UA Vs’ ﬂight distance.
Given the varying emergency levels at different geohazard
monitoring points, we assign different importance levels to
the SNs. When UA Vs cannot access all SNs in one task, we
prioritize data collection from those with higher importance
levels. In emergencies, prompt transmission of this data to theBS is critical. To facilitate this, we propose a Gaussian mixture
clustering algorithm based on fuzzy thresholds, which divides
the sensor network into multiple subnets. This algorithm cate-gorizes SNs within each subnet into two types based on fuzzy
thresholds: 1) deterministic SNs, which have ﬁxed cluster

C. UAV Scheduling Charging
Due to the limited battery capacity of UA Vs, they cannot
continuously perform data collection tasks. Therefore, we have
equipped the ground BS with a UA V charging spot. When a
UA V’s energy is insufﬁcient to complete the remaining datacollection tasks, it can return to the BS for recharging. In
traditional UA V-assisted IoT systems, UA Vs typically collect
data from SNs within their current communication range and
relay it to the BS. They then move to the next position to
continue data collection and relaying. This process is repeateduntil UA Vs are forced to return to the BS for charging due
to low battery. However, this data collection and recharging
method is inefﬁcient. First, once UA Vs’ batteries are depleted,they take a long time to fully charge. Second, if the BS has
limited charging spots available for UA Vs and there is a lackof effective communication and coordination among UA Vs, it

VI. C
ONCLUSION
For geohazard monitoring scenarios, this article proposes a
LLDCL. This article assigns different importance levels to SNsand introduces a DRL-WAoI to achieve dynamic path planning
for UA Vs in geohazard monitoring scenarios. Additionally,
this article establishes a token-based energy trading model
between UA Vs and the BS to facilitate efﬁcient UA V recharg-
ing. Simulation experiments show that, compared to existingdata collection schemes, this proposed scheme demonstrates
superior performance in terms of system AoI while ensuring
low latency and high importance of data. In this work, wemainly focus on scenarios with a single BS. However, due
to the typically large areas of geohazard monitoring areas,a single BS often cannot cover all affected areas. Moreover,
when geohazards occur in geographically challenging envi-ronments, such as forests and valleys, the ﬂight areas for
UA Vs may be restricted. Therefore, future research can focus
on collaborative data collection and obstacle avoidance pathplanning for UA Vs in multiple BS scenarios.
